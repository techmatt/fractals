Rendering modes

Rendering fundamentals built one way of rendering a location: compute the smooth field, percentile-stretch it, map a palette across it. There are several other ways to render a fractal, each reaching for a different artistic effect, and this is hardly an exhaustive set: dedicated fractal-art programs like Ultra Fractal carry far more options than what is shown here. A rendering mode is any recipe for turning a location's orbits into a picture, and because an orbit is a whole path through the complex plane, dozens or thousands of positions of which the escape time keeps just one number, there are many recipes to choose from, each with its own artistic character. Most modes work exactly like smooth does: reduce each orbit to a value, collect the values into a field, and hand the field to the same stretching and palette machinery as before. But not all of them. Some blend two renderings into one, some shift the palette by where the orbit went, and some skip fields entirely and paint the orbit's behavior directly, which is roughly the order they arrive in below.

I tried a wide range of the rendering techniques people have developed for these objects and picked my favorites, shown in the figure below. The rest of this page goes through those modes in detail, with the approximate parameter ranges I picked for each. Most figures here show a location twice, the smooth rendering on the left and the mode on the right, at the same location in the same palette, so whatever changes between the two panels is the mode and nothing else.

Fields from the orbit's path

Three modes keep smooth's recipe, one number per orbit and one continuous field, but measure some property of the path instead of the clock.

Triangle-inequality average (tia) checks, at every step, where the orbit actually landed inside the range it could possibly have landed in. Before the step, the triangle inequality brackets the next distance from the origin between a lowest and highest possible value; the mode scores the real landing as a fraction between those bounds, and averages the fractions over the orbit. Because the score can swing fully between one step and the next, the field bands far more finely than escape time: the result reads as engraving where smooth reads as contour lines.

Stripe coloring works from the angles of the orbit's points. Each orbit point is a complex number and so has an angle, and the mode averages a wave over those angles: one-half plus one-half the sine of six times the angle, six being the stripe count the pipeline draws with. The wave's crests show up as flowing bands laid along the fractal's structure. As with the other orbit averages here, the final term is blended in by the fractional part of the smooth count from rendering fundamentals, so that crossing a whole escape step does not put a terrace of its own into the picture.

Curvature coloring measures how sharply the path turns: at each step, the unsigned angle between the new step and the one before it, averaged along the orbit. Orbits that turn differently separate cleanly in the image even when they escape at nearly the same moment, which is a distinction escape time makes nothing of, and one that survives into parts of the frame where the smooth field has gone flat.

Orbit traps

An orbit trap places a fixed shape in the complex plane and asks each orbit how close it ever came to the shape. No trap is drawn as a mode of its own in the roster below; traps appear in two roles, as a texture laid over a smooth rendering in the composites, and painted directly in the last family on the page. Two shapes carry that work. The cross trap is the distance to the two coordinate axes, crossing at the origin. The Gaussian-integer trap traps against the grid of points with whole-number real and imaginary parts, so it is not one shape at a center at all: a single trap cell repeats across the entire plane, and the stamped echoes tile instead of radiating, a texture with the granularity of graph paper crumpled along the fractal's folds. And unlike every field mode above, a trap is defined for interior points too, which is what fills the normally-black lake with structure where a trap's own reading is used as a texture.

Blending modes together

A composite is also a rendering mode. The engine can render the same location twice, a base and a texture, normalize each, and blend them with any of the standard picture-blending operators (multiply, screen, additive, and their relatives). Five of the modes the pipeline draws are built this way, all on a smooth base. Four of them blend with screen at 85% strength. smooth_stripe and smooth_curvature lay their namesake fields over the smooth rendering. smooth_angle_min lays over the angle of the iterate at the orbit's closest approach to the Gaussian-integer trap. smooth_mean_angle reads something less obvious: it takes the orbit's nearest, mean and farthest distances to that same lattice and turns the imbalance among those three into an angle, so what it draws is how lopsided the orbit's approaches were rather than which direction they came from. The 85% is a taste constant with a reason: at full strength the texture's own normalization competes with the base's and the escape structure stops being legible underneath.

The fifth, threads, is the family's extremist. Its texture accumulates the cross trap over the whole orbit: every pass near the axes contributes, weighted by a narrow bell around the trap, so a pixel whose orbit grazed the axes a dozen times reads differently from one that grazed once. The result is laid additively over the smooth base at half strength, giving sparse bright filaments that trace the orbit's near-misses and stack on top of the classic rendering rather than mixing into it. Additive blending is why the filaments glow. It is also expensive to draw: the trap distance and its kernel are evaluated on every iteration the orbit runs, so the cost lands hardest exactly where orbits run longest.

The itinerary mode

The itinerary mode comes from a classical idea in the study of dynamics: instead of asking when an orbit escapes, ask where it goes on the way. Cut the plane into four equal angular sectors around the origin, follow the orbit, and write down the sequence of sectors it visits, its route recorded coarsely, then read that sequence as digits after the point in base four, so the first few moves dominate the value and each later move refines it. The result carves the image into territories of shared history: within a patch every orbit opened with the same route, and the borders between patches are where the dynamics start to disagree, so they are hard-edged, and the image reads as stained glass where smooth reads as airbrush. The itinerary does not form a field of its own; it shifts the palette. The smooth rendering is drawn underneath, and each pixel's address rotates its palette position by up to half a turn, so the territories appear as coordinated recolorings of the classic structure. (On Julia-set-style images, where every pixel is its own starting point, the engine reads the route from the first iterated point rather than the pixel itself, since otherwise the leading digit would just cut the picture into quadrants.)

Direct traps

The last family never makes a field at all. A direct trap watches each orbit as it iterates, and every time an iterate falls within a threshold of the trap shape, it takes a sample from the palette and paints it into the pixel then and there. How close the iterate came decides both where in the gradient the sample is taken and how much of it lands, which is why the strokes come out soft-edged with nothing extra to tune. A pixel's final color is the stack of every near-miss its orbit ever made, in order.

Three modes ship this way, built from two shapes. The cross of the coordinate axes carries two of them, once brightening a black ground and once darkening a white one, and the two read nothing alike. The third is the real axis alone, the family's one directional member. Because nothing is normalized per frame, direct traps are the only modes that don't adapt to the location they're pointed at: where near-misses are sparse they are spectacular, and where near-misses are everywhere they wash out.

The modes the pipeline draws

One structural fact shapes everything: the search for good locations (finding good locations) renders only smooth. Every other mode enters later, in finding good wallpapers, when the pipeline takes a discovered location and tries renderings of it: one attempt in smooth and two in other modes, drawn without replacement from the rest of the roster.

Why search over modes at all, rather than pick the best one and use it everywhere? Because a mode that works great for one location fails spectacularly at the next, and good smooth geometry is no protection. The table below shows, for each mode, how the judge's ratings of every candidate the pipeline has rendered spread across the four quality tiers from training judges, counting only locations whose smooth rendering the judge rates a three or better, so every row starts from geometry that already works. The scores are the judge's rather than mine: nobody has looked at a hundred thousand pictures, and the judge's own readings run optimistic against the ratings it was trained on, so a row is a machine's estimate of my taste and not my taste itself. Even so, most of these modes spend most of their attempts at a one or a two, and some, like curvature, need roughly 30 attempts to find one strong result. That is the reason to try several modes at every location and keep whatever survives. Beyond this catalog

The wider fractal-art world has accumulated many more ways to render these objects, and a few come up often enough to deserve a name and a pointer:

  • Distance estimation tracks, alongside each orbit, how sensitively its endpoint responds to nudging the pixel, and converts that into an estimate of the pixel's distance to the set's boundary. Rendered directly, the boundary becomes crisp filaments of uniform width at any zoom, like a technical drawing of the fractal. The renderer implements this one and will draw it on request; it is simply not among the modes the wallpaper search draws from.
  • Normal-map lighting treats a field as terrain: read its slope, convert the slope to a surface direction, and light the fractal as relief carved in material, the same normal mapping trick games use to shade detailed surfaces cheaply. The engine leaves this one out on purpose rather than for want of the code: it makes a fractal read as an embossed metal plaque instead of as a field, and the picture's own structure gets replaced by a lighting model's.
  • The Buddhabrot abandons per-pixel coloring entirely: it follows the full orbits of millions of escaping points and brightens every pixel each orbit passes through, building a glowing density image of where the dynamics actually travel, something like an X-ray of the Mandelbrot set.
  • Histogram coloring maps field values by how often each value occurs rather than by its raw size, so every color gets used in proportion to how much of the image wants it. It solves the same squeezed-range problem as the percentile stretch from rendering fundamentals, but reads the whole distribution rather than two points on it.
  • Fractal flames leave escape-time fractals altogether: they iterate a system of nonlinear functions and accumulate the wandering point's path into a log-scaled density image, colored by which functions the path took, a different mathematical object with its own dedicated tools.
  • Programs like Ultra Fractal treat all of this as an open library: thousands of user-written coloring algorithms, freely combined with any fractal formula, stacked in Photoshop-style layers, a reminder that a rendering mode is just a program, and there is no end of programs to write.

One discovery, many wallpapers

Finding a genuinely good location is the uncertain part of the pipeline, and once found, a location is not one image. It is a smooth render, a tia render, a threads render, each in any palette: one discovery, many possible wallpapers, and the modes multiply what every discovery is worth. Finding good locations covers how the pool of locations gets built, training judges covers teaching a machine to recognize when the render that came back is worth keeping, and finding good wallpapers is where a found location is drawn again and again across the roster on this page.
