Deep zoom

Every picture in this project's main galleries is computed in double-precision floating point, the 64-bit number format a processor does arithmetic in natively. This is fast and efficient, but it breaks down when you zoom deeply enough into the Mandelbrot set. Past a certain point, neighboring samples round to the same number and whole groups of pixels compute the same orbit. The result is blocky discretization artifacts that have nothing to do with the fractal, and they get worse the deeper you go.

It's possible, but expensive, to switch to higher-precision arithmetic: 128-bit floats, or arbitrary-precision libraries such as rug in Rust or mpmath in Python. Fortunately, the deep-zoom community has developed clever mathematics that looks much deeper into the Mandelbrot set far more efficiently. I go over it briefly at the end of this page. Deeper frames also need a slower, more deliberate way of browsing, which the Deep tab of the fractal explorer provides.

Here is a video of a deep descent, down to a frame about 3.5 × 10−15 wide. It was made with builder/zoom.py, which computes a high-precision field once at each halving of the width and colors it separately.

Deep frames have wildly varying escape times, and getting a rendering that conveys their full range of detail often takes more exploration and parameter tweaking. The percentile stretch from rendering fundamentals spreads the palette evenly over each frame's own values. At this depth escape counts run into the tens of thousands, and the stretch leaves most of the picture inside a few percent of the palette. For deep frames an absolute scale generally works best: the smooth count passes through a logarithm or a power curve, and the palette then repeats at a fixed period. What depth adds to a picture

With some deliberate choices guiding the descent, deeper parts of the Mandelbrot set and related fractals produce much richer detail and pattern in regions that look plain and smooth at shallower depths. The figure below compares a shallow frame with a deep frame inside it.

Small copies of the set, minibrots, are everywhere along the boundary: every filament passes infinitely many of them, at every scale. From outside, a copy looks like a small black body with the familiar bulbs and hairs. A frame that zooms into the copy's neighborhood sees more than that. It finds the copy's own seahorse valleys and spirals laid over the structure it was already in, so a region that looked smooth from further out fills with filigree. The strip below follows one descent of this kind, five steps down a chain of components nested one inside the next. Each step frames the next component in the chain, and the last frame is about a billionth of the complex plane wide.

Satellite copies and the seahorse valley

The narrow gap between the main cardioid and the large disk to its left is the seahorse valley, named for the curled shapes that line it. Wikipedia's zoom-sequence gallery walks down it: double spirals on one side, seahorses on the other, and at the heart of the pattern a small distorted copy of the set, which it calls a satellite. Around a satellite the seahorse tails form rings, and the number of tails in each ring grows in powers of two. The video's path is a second instance of the same walk. It enters the valley, passes its double spiral, reaches a copy, and then repeats the sequence inside that copy's own seahorse valley, several decades deeper. The stills below are taken from that path.

Embedded Julia sets

The repetition has a reason. Near a copy whose orbit has period p, taking p steps of the map at once behaves, to a good approximation, like a single step of a fresh quadratic map. So the neighborhood of the copy reproduces the structure of the whole set, slightly distorted. The same mechanism makes Julia sets appear inside the Mandelbrot set. As the video approaches its copy it passes a two-fold shape, then a four-fold one, then an eight-fold one, each twice as symmetric as the last, before the copy's black body comes into view. These are embedded Julia sets. Wikipedia's gallery states which Julia set they imitate: the one for the parameter that sits in the same position relative to the whole set as the frame sits relative to the copy. The target of this zoom sits in its copy's seahorse valley but just outside the copy itself, so that parameter lies outside the set, and its Julia set is a cloud of disconnected dust. That is the shape the stages break into as the zoom closes in. The panel beside the strip shows that Julia set for comparison, and the explorer's Julia preview shows the Julia set under the pointer for any parameter.

How a deep frame is computed

The Deep tab computes one orbit at high precision, the reference orbit, for the center of the frame, using fixed-point arithmetic with as many bits as the frame's width requires. Every pixel then iterates only its small offset from that orbit. Write a pixel's orbit as zn = Zn + δn, where Zn is the reference orbit, and let Δc be the pixel's offset in parameter. For z ↦ z2 + c the offset then obeys

δn+1 = (2Zn + δn)δn + Δc

and the higher degrees follow from the binomial expansion in the same way. The offsets are tiny, but a double keeps its relative precision at any magnitude, so δ fits comfortably in an ordinary double even where the coordinates themselves do not. This is perturbation, and it is how every modern deep-zoom renderer works.

The reference orbit is only a good anchor while a pixel's orbit stays near it. When the full value Zn + δn passes closer to zero than δn itself, the offset has become the larger quantity and precision starts to drain away. The fix used here is rebasing, due to Zhuoran on fractalforums.org. At that step the pixel takes its whole current value as a new offset and restarts from the beginning of the same reference orbit. With rebasing, one reference orbit serves the entire frame. The tab draws the multibrot families up to degree six, on both the parameter and dynamical planes, in the smooth rendering. Its iteration cap follows the same depth rule as the rest of the engine, up to a ceiling of a million, and a probe raises it when too many samples reach it. In practice it works down to widths of about 10−30 close to a minibrot, where the ceiling on iterations runs out first, and to about 10−50 elsewhere, where a coordinate outgrows the 64 characters its link can carry. Renderers that also extend the exponent range of their offsets go hundreds of decades deeper.

The tab runs on the CPU, in WebAssembly, across the browser's worker threads. On one six-core desktop, the video's final frame takes about 15 seconds at 1280 by 720 with one sample per pixel, averaging about fifteen thousand iterations a sample. On the same machine, Fraktaler 3, a mature open-source deep-zoom renderer, takes about 50 seconds on the CPU with its acceleration on and about 100 without it. Compared like for like (CPU, doubles, plain perturbation), the tab does about seven times as many iterations per second. The likely reason is specialization rather than a better algorithm: Fraktaler 3 interprets general formulas and tracks derivatives at every step, while this kernel runs one fixed update. The two agree on more than 99.7% of pixels.

GPUs are clearly faster at this kind of independent per-pixel work. On the same machine Fraktaler 3's GPU path draws the frame in under 3 seconds, in single precision. Consumer GPUs are slow at doubles, and in double precision the same GPU roughly ties the tab's CPU kernel, which is why the fast GPU renderers work in single precision with extended-exponent tricks. This project kept rendering on the CPU for portability, and used the GPU only to evaluate its neural networks.

Deep-zoom renderers usually add bilinear approximation, which skips many iterations at once by applying a precomputed linear map. The tab does not use it, and the reason is worth knowing for anyone building one. On frames with visible structure, the kind a reader opens, it bought well under a factor of three. At every tolerance tried it also moved escape counts, and on some frames it moved the boundary of the interior, so the pictures stopped matching what the engine draws. It paid off handsomely only on frames that were almost entirely interior. Fraktaler 3 shows the same pattern on the video's final frame: its approximation buys a factor of about 1.3 to 2 and moves a fifth of the pixels by more than one iteration.

A gallery of deep frames

The Deep tab carries a small gallery of frames found this way. The first set came from a rough search. It starts in two places: the video's own final frame, recentred on the small copies found near its end, and places the location search had already found inside a small copy of the set. From each it descends: at every step it searches the view for the nuclei of smaller copies, takes the largest that is clearly smaller than the last, and frames it. The views between one copy and the next supply the symmetry stages, and zooming into the busiest parts of earlier frames supplies most of the spirals and seahorses. The Julia-plane frames map a familiar parameter into a deep copy, through the copy's own scale and rotation, and draw the Julia set there. A Claude Code session narrowed about 140 low-resolution previews to 65, and I picked the gallery's first 31 from those by eye.

The search is rough because searching here is expensive. A candidate for the wallpaper pipeline is cheap to render and is screened by a trained judge. A deep frame takes tens of seconds to minutes even at a modest size, and nothing judges it. A search that renders broadly and filters afterward cannot afford that breadth at these depths, so a better deep search would need to know more precisely where it is going before it spends a render: which copies to descend into, and where around them the interesting structure lies.

Every figure on this page links to its frame in the explorer, the deep ones in the Deep tab, and the tab's links carry the frame and its coloring.
