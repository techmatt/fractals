Overview

For many years my desktop background has cycled through a collection of fractal wallpapers gathered from around the internet — but seeing the same wallpapers over and over grew a bit repetitive, and my daughter was fascinated specifically by dark pink fractals, so I've spent some time thinking about writing a program to automatically find, colorize, and stylize fractals. A great fractal wallpaper is the product of several artistic stages — choosing a location and a framing, picking and aligning palettes, stylizing, layered post-processing — craft that is usually done by hand, in tools like UltraFractal or Chaotica. This project doesn't try to match that level of hand-tuned artistry on any single image. The goal is a system that produces a rich and growing set of good wallpapers, spanning a wide range of structures, fractal families, color styles, and rendering effects. Drawing a fractal turns out to be only a tiny part of that. This site goes over how we decide where to point the camera, how to draw what it sees, and which of the results are worth keeping. Everything on this site lives in the flat complex plane — two-dimensional escape-time fractals. There are other beautiful fractal worlds this project doesn't touch, among them the 3D Mandelbulb, fractal flames, and quaternion Julia sets rendered in four dimensions.

Everything here is backed by a working open-source repository, fractal-wallpapers, and every image on this site came out of it. The same renderer is also compiled to run in a browser, so the fractal explorer draws these fractals live on this site — every family, each rendering mode the article describes, and a broad selection of palettes, panned and zoomed by hand — and most pictures on these pages carry a link that opens the explorer at exactly the view you are looking at. The finished wallpapers will be published here as galleries, free to download at full resolution. You can also run the project yourself, use the trained models and the labeled datasets behind them, or read on for the technical details.

Making this work means answering three genuinely interesting questions, and the system is organized around them. Where in an infinite plane do you point the camera — and how far do you zoom — to find promising material? Given a location — a center and a width in the complex plane — which of many rendering styles and palettes will suit it? And out of thousands of finished candidates, which set is worth keeping? Each question is one part of a pipeline, and each part feeds the next:

Finding good locations — a guided walk through each fractal family descends toward structure and away from both emptiness and pure chaos, scoring neighborhoods as it goes with a small neural network trained on my ratings of what makes promising wallpaper material. What it keeps is a growing pool of locations, each recorded as a frame in the complex plane and nothing more — no colors yet, no style (training judges).

Finding good wallpapers — each location in the pool is then drawn many ways: in several rendering modes, from the classic smooth coloring to stranger styles, and under a spread of palettes, with the picture's tones balanced automatically. A second neural network, trained on my ratings of finished pictures, scores each attempt, and every attempt joins a pool of candidate wallpapers with its score attached (rendering fundamentals, rendering modes, color palettes).

Gallery curation — a gallery of any size is chosen out of that pool, for quality first and then under constraints on the set as a whole: no near-duplicates of one view, no single family of color crowding out the rest, every rendering mode represented.

The full pipeline page shows the three parts running end to end.

Two ideas run through all three parts. The neural networks — the judges — stand in for my taste at every point where a machine has to decide whether something is good; I rated by my own wallpaper preferences, and fine-tuning toward other tastes would be straightforward. And what the project finds, it keeps: the location pool only grows, and the best candidates at every location are kept, so the curation part can always be re-run over everything the project has ever found.

Each section is written to be readable independently. Nothing here requires more than a single desktop machine with one ordinary GPU. The whole system — search, rendering, training, curation — was built to run unattended on fairly standard hardware. Every candidate the pipeline judges is drawn small; a wallpaper that ships is rendered again on a larger frame, with several samples to a pixel, and re-rendering the collection at a different resolution is easy.
