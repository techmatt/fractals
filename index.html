Making Fractal Wallpapers

Rendering escape-time fractals fast, walking the plane for places worth rendering, and teaching small models to judge what comes back.

The article is being written section by section — completed sections are marked below; the rest show the outline. The code is in fractal-wallpapers, and finished wallpapers are in the galleries.

Contents

  1. Overview ✓

    A pipeline that searches fractals for views worth rendering and turns the best into wallpapers — and why the finding, not the drawing, is the hard part.

  2. Escape-time fractals ✓

    The four families behind every picture here — Mandelbrot, the multibrots, Julia, Phoenix — what it means to iterate a formula on the complex plane, and how a "location" specifies one exact view into a set.

  3. Rendering fundamentals ✓

    How an image is actually computed: turning integer escape counts into a smooth field, choosing the iteration cap and the number of samples a pixel deserves, and fitting the palette to the frame rather than to the arithmetic.

  4. Rendering modes

    Different ways to draw the same escape data: hard bands versus smooth gradients, styles built from the orbit's whole path rather than just its exit, and the contrast stretch that fits raw values to an image.

  5. Finding good locations

    The automated search: walks that zoom into the plane one step at a time, tests that decide whether a frame is worth keeping, and a record of every candidate — kept or not.

  6. Training judges

    Collecting human judgments of real renders and training small networks on them: one scores locations, two judge finished renders, one picks palettes.

  7. Color palettes

    Palettes as data: how one is chosen to suit each image, and how its tones are corrected against the range measured from wallpapers that already work.

  8. From locations to wallpapers

    The final selection: scoring every surviving candidate, choosing a small set across families and styles, and writing down why each picture was released, rejected, or passed over.

  9. Running at scale

    What multi-hour runs require: measuring how rare good material is in each family, putting a compute price on each find, and splitting the time budget so scarce families still get their share.

  10. Deep zoom

    Past the limits of double precision: why ordinary arithmetic fails at extreme magnification, and the perturbation trick that keeps rendering possible far beyond it.
