Making Fractal Wallpapers

This site is about rendering escape-time fractals quickly, searching the complex plane for the views worth rendering, and training small models to judge what comes back. The article is being written section by section — completed sections are marked below; the rest show the outline. The code is in fractal-wallpapers, and finished wallpapers are in the galleries.

Contents

  1. Overview ✓

    What the project makes, and how a program finds views worth rendering and turns the best of them into wallpapers. Drawing a fractal is the easy half; deciding where to point the camera is the hard one.

  2. Escape-time fractals ✓

    Iterating one short formula on the complex plane draws every picture on this site. This section covers the four families the project uses — Mandelbrot, the multibrots, Julia and Phoenix — and what it takes to name one exact view inside one of them.

  3. Rendering fundamentals ✓

    A render starts as a grid of integer escape counts. This section turns those counts into a smooth field, works out how long to iterate and how many samples a pixel deserves, and fits the palette to the frame rather than to the arithmetic.

  4. Rendering modes ✓

    The escape count keeps one number out of a whole orbit, and there are many other ways to reduce that orbit to a color. This section works through the catalog, from styles measured along the orbit's path to the four that paint while the iteration is still running.

  5. Finding good locations ✓

    Point a camera at random into a fractal and almost everything you get back is mediocre at best. This section is about what separates a view worth keeping from the rest, and about the guided walk that goes looking — drawing each candidate cheaply, throwing most of them away, and navigating by the miniature copies of the set that good views tend to sit near.

  6. Training judges

    Whether a wallpaper is any good is a human verdict. This section collects those verdicts at a scale a model can learn from and trains four small judges on them — one scores a location, two score a finished render, one picks a palette.

  7. Color palettes

    A colormap is drawn without knowing which fractal it will be spent on, so the same palette that suits one render leaves the next muddy. This section chooses a palette to suit each image and corrects its tones against the range measured from wallpapers that already work.

  8. From locations to wallpapers

    A run ends with far more candidates than anyone wants. This section scores every one of them, keeps a small set that spans families and styles, and writes down why each picture was released, rejected or passed over.

  9. Running at scale

    Good material is far rarer in some families than in others, so an hour of compute buys very different amounts depending on where it is spent. This section measures how rare, puts a price on each find, and splits a long run's budget so the scarce families still get their share.

  10. Deep zoom

    Zoom far enough and two neighboring samples round to the same number, and the arithmetic an ordinary renderer runs in stops telling them apart. This section finds where that wall actually sits, works right up against it, and is honest about what a picture drawn there can and cannot be trusted to say.
